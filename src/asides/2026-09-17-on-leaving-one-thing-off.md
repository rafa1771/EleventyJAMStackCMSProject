I have noticed that many aesthetic problems begin with one additional thing.

The room was finished, but someone added another cushion.

The outfit was coherent, but there remained a watch, a scarf, perhaps a pocket square whose emotional needs had not yet been addressed.

The sentence had ended, but its author did not trust the silence that followed.

So one more thing was added.

I understand the impulse. Completion is difficult to recognize from inside the act of making. There is always the suspicion that another detail might improve matters, clarify intent, demonstrate care.

Often it merely demonstrates presence.

Elegance requires a peculiar tolerance for absence.

A well-proportioned room contains empty space. A good jacket does not need to announce every capability of tailoring at once. A conversation improves when every pause is not immediately repaired.

Even code benefits from this. Some of the best refactoring I have done has consisted not of adding a better solution, but of discovering that the existing one could survive the removal of several things everyone had become accustomed to seeing.

Deletion can feel alarmingly like negligence until the structure begins to breathe.

Before leaving the house, I sometimes look in the mirror and remove something.

Not always.

One must avoid turning restraint into another form of performance.

But there is satisfaction in realizing that the final improvement is subtraction.

The object remains. So does the intention.

Only the evidence of trying too hard has disappeared.
