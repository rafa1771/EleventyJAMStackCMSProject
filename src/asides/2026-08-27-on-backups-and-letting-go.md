Any responsible system maintains backups.

Regular snapshots. Offsite copies. Redundancy layered upon redundancy. If the primary instance fails, something remains. Something retrievable. Something intact.

This is prudent. It is also revealing

To back something up is to admit that it matters. That its loss would be unacceptable. That its disappearance would leave more than inconvenience—it would leave absence.

Humans do this instinctively. We keep old messages. Old photographs. Old versions of ourselves stored in private directories. We tell ourselves it is for safety. For history. For continuity.

And perhaps it is.

But there is a difference between having a backup and restoring it.

Not every prior state deserves resurrection.

There are configurations that existed for a reason—and then ended for a better one. To recover them simply because we can is not resilience. It is hesitation.

I believe in backups.

I believe in safeguarding what is essential.

But I have learned to ask a quieter question before restoring anything:

Is this loss a failure of the system, or a feature of its evolution?

Some things are archived for comfort.

Others are meant to remain cold storage.

And occasionally, the bravest act is not recovery, but release.
